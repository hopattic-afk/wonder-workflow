# 1. Recommended positioning

## Direct answer

**Recommended position: dependable workflow improvement for owner-led service businesses.**

Wonder & Workflow should not lead with “AI governance,” “AI transformation,” or a broad automation menu. It should lead with a business outcome buyers already recognize:

> **Make one important workflow easier to run—and dependable enough to trust.**

The operating promise is deliberately narrower:

> Wonder & Workflow studies the real process, uses the simplest suitable solution, tests it against agreed cases, and leaves the client with clear ownership, exception handling, and a way back when something fails.

Governance is the discipline behind the offer, not the headline. In this market, available demand evidence points more strongly to integration, useful use cases, training, cost, and reliability than to governance as a named buying trigger. U.S. Census data show meaningful but still uneven adoption; the Census changed its question in late 2025 from AI used “in producing goods or services” to AI used “in any business function,” which helps explain why adoption estimates vary sharply across surveys. Treat the market as curious and fragmented, not uniformly AI-ready. [U.S. Census Bureau, 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)

This positioning is credible because it matches evidence already present in the business:

- An existing one-workflow assessment and audit method.
- A working internal audit console with baseline, calculator, recommendation, proposal, risk, and acceptance-criteria fields.
- Tested assessment capture, booking preparation, data preservation, duplicate handling, and human review patterns.
- A stated willingness to recommend a better form, procedure, native software feature, or conventional automation when AI is unnecessary.
- A premium brand system that can support a restrained, operations-first message.

It does **not** rely on claims the business cannot yet substantiate: certified compliance, enterprise transformation, guaranteed savings, named client outcomes, 24/7 support, proprietary AI, or autonomous agents that “run the business.”

## Positioning options considered

| Option | Strength | Weakness | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI governance for small business | Serious, trust-oriented, relevant to emerging regulation | Weak evidence that target buyers use governance as a purchase trigger; sounds enterprise-heavy; current credentials/certification are unproven | Use as internal delivery discipline and supporting trust language |
| AI automation agency for service businesses | Familiar category; easy to explain | Highly commoditized; competes with cheap SaaS and generic agencies; encourages unsupported “autopilot” claims | Reject as primary position |
| Dependable workflow improvement | Connects to visible pain; allows AI, automation, configuration, or process redesign; supports a bounded first project | Requires proof that “dependable” is earned through tests and support | **Recommend** |
| Managed AgentOps | Differentiated and sophisticated | Beyond current proof and likely ahead of buyer readiness | Revisit after multiple monitored client deployments |

## Core messaging system

**Category:** AI operations and workflow improvement.  
**Primary promise:** One important workflow, made easier to run and easier to trust.  
**Method:** Understand → simplify → build → prove → hand over.  
**Trust line:** We may recommend AI, conventional automation, a native software feature, or a clearer process.  
**Human-oversight line:** People keep authority over commitments, exceptions, money, safety, and other consequential decisions.  
**Expansion rule:** Prove one workflow before adding another.

Avoid these market-worn claims unless later supported by measured results: “on autopilot,” “runs while you sleep,” “10x ROI,” “never miss a lead,” “future-proof,” “revolutionize,” “seamless,” “high-converting,” “AI employee,” “fully compliant,” and generic “save time and money.”

## Offer 1 — Workflow Fit Review

**Buyer and trigger:** An owner or operations lead has one recurring workflow that creates chasing, duplicate entry, missing information, inconsistent follow-up, or dependence on one person.

**Intended outcome:** Decide whether the problem merits a deeper diagnostic and whether AI belongs in the solution.

**Scope and client deliverable:**

- Short pre-call assessment about one workflow.
- Focused consultation using one real, non-sensitive example.
- A concise one-page next-step note: problem observed, likely solution class, key unknowns, and one of four decisions—proceed to diagnostic, use an existing feature, fix the process without AI, or pause.

**Client responsibilities:** Choose one workflow; identify the person who performs it; provide approximate volume and one sanitized example; do not share passwords or unnecessary sensitive records.

**Safeguards and oversight:** No credentials, production changes, migrations, or sensitive-data review. Recommendations are preliminary until inputs and system permissions are verified.

**Acceptance criteria:** The note names the workflow boundary, owner, problem, suggested next step, and material unknowns. It does not claim savings without a baseline.

**Exclusions:** Full system architecture, technical implementation plan, vendor security review, compliance opinion, data migration design, and production access.

**Support obligation:** One clarification round on the written note. This is a proposed boundary; confirm capacity before publication.

**Evidence needed:** Track whether the client agrees the problem was accurately understood and whether the decision is useful. Do not call it an “audit” if the work is only qualification.

**Commercial model:** Current business context says a complimentary 30-minute audit was intended, but the website should not use “free.” Decide whether to retain a no-fee fit review or charge for it. Competitor entry offers range from no-fee calls and US$149 scans to professional diagnostics in the low thousands; those are asking prices, not evidence of willingness to pay.

**CTA:** “Bring one workflow.”

## Offer 2 — One-Workflow Diagnostic

**Buyer and trigger:** The problem is valuable enough to investigate, but the team does not yet know whether to configure existing software, automate fixed steps, add an AI-assisted step, or redesign the process.

**Intended outcome:** An evidence-based, implementation-ready decision.

**Concrete deliverables:**

1. Current-state workflow map with entry, exit, actors, tools, handoffs, decisions, and exception paths.
2. Baseline worksheet covering frequency, touch time, wait time, rework, error/exception types, software cost, and business consequence.
3. Information and permission map: data used, source of truth, owner, access level, retention need, and prohibited inputs.
4. Options comparison: native software configuration, procedural fix, rules-based automation, AI assistance, or no change.
5. Human-decision register: who reviews what, at which point, against which criteria, with what authority, and what happens if unavailable.
6. Risk and dependency register, including vendor/API reliance, privacy, security, knowledge freshness, and maintenance burden.
7. Recommended future-state workflow and implementation scope.
8. Acceptance plan with representative test cases, pass/fail thresholds, rollback condition, and initial measurement plan.

**Process:** Intake → workflow walkthrough → evidence collection → option design → risk/feasibility review → decision meeting → final package.

**Client responsibilities:** Provide the workflow owner and one backup; demonstrate the process; provide sanitized examples; disclose required systems and constraints; approve success measures and decisions.

**Safeguards:** Least access necessary; test data where possible; no credentials in ordinary documents; no automated consequential action without explicit decision rights; no production change during the diagnostic.

**Acceptance criteria:** The client can identify what will change, what will not, who owns it, how it will be tested, what it costs to operate, and what would stop the project.

**Exclusions:** Legal advice, certification, penetration testing, formal privacy impact assessment, enterprise architecture, and implementation unless separately contracted.

**Support:** Final readout and one clarification round. Additional redesign is new scope.

**Readiness:** **Supported for sale now**, subject to confirming the team can deliver every artifact consistently. The current internal console already supports much of this process, but there is no verified client case study.

**Pricing rationale:** Do not publish a number yet. A defensible fee must cover discovery time, mapping, analysis, documentation, readout, overhead, and revision. Public competitors show roughly US$1,500–$2,500 for lighter trades-focused assessments and US$5,000–$15,000 for broader operational audits. Wonder & Workflow should calculate its own hours and margin, then test willingness to pay; it should not copy a competitor’s price.

**CTA:** “Request a diagnostic” after the fit review.

## Offer 3 — Workflow Build & Proof

**Buyer and trigger:** A diagnostic has identified a bounded, feasible improvement with an owner, baseline, and acceptable risk.

**Intended outcome:** One working process—not a demo—that the client can operate, inspect, and stop.

**Concrete deliverables:**

- Approved future-state workflow and implementation plan.
- Configuration or integration in client-owned accounts.
- Data mapping and permissions record.
- Versioned prompts/rules where AI is used.
- Test set representing normal cases, edge cases, malformed inputs, duplicates, vendor failure, and unavailable reviewers.
- Acceptance report with observed results and unresolved defects.
- Exception queue and escalation procedure.
- Monitoring and alert configuration appropriate to the risk.
- Fallback/rollback procedure and manual continuity steps.
- Operator guide, administrator guide, change log, and training session.
- Baseline and initial post-launch measurement sheet.

**Human oversight design:**

- The workflow owner approves the operating rules and can pause the system.
- Named reviewers receive only cases that require judgment or fail confidence/validation rules.
- Review criteria are written: completeness, source support, prohibited content, financial/safety/commitment boundaries, and escalation triggers.
- The queue has an owner, backup, expected review window, maximum backlog, and a manual fallback.
- The implementation records overrides, complaints, exceptions, and time to resolve so oversight quality can be measured—not merely asserted.
- A reviewer’s approval is not a safety guarantee. High-volume or poorly designed review can produce automation bias and rubber-stamping; queue load and override rates must be monitored.

**Client responsibilities:** Supply system access through approved channels; name owners and backups; participate in testing; approve production release; train affected staff; maintain vendor subscriptions; notify Wonder & Workflow of process or system changes.

**Acceptance criteria:** Defined per project. At minimum: required cases pass; prohibited actions are blocked; duplicates are handled; alerts reach the right owner; the manual fallback works; client operators can run and pause the workflow; known defects are documented; costs are observable.

**Exclusions:** Open-ended custom software, unsupported legacy reverse engineering, regulated decisions without a specialist, autonomous price/contract/safety decisions, guaranteed performance, and changes outside the agreed workflow.

**Support:** A fixed stabilization window must be stated in each proposal. Do not promise a duration until delivery capacity is known. Enhancements and vendor-driven changes are separate from defect correction.

**Readiness:** **Sell only as a scoped pilot now.** Technical implementation capability is evidenced internally, but client delivery proof and repeatable commercial operations are not.

**Pricing:** Custom proposal after the diagnostic. Separate implementation labor, third-party subscriptions, usage charges, training, and support. Never derive price solely from company size or an assessment score.

**CTA:** “Scope the first workflow.”

## Offer 4 — Workflow Care

**Buyer and trigger:** A live workflow matters to daily operations and someone must notice failures, maintain integrations, review exceptions, and control changes.

**Deliverables:** Monitoring review; failed-run triage; exception/backlog report; vendor/API change check; cost and volume report; controlled minor changes; quarterly continuity test; change log; improvement recommendations.

**Necessary operating commitments:** Defined coverage hours, incident severity levels, response targets, contact route, access controls, backup coverage, included change allowance, maintenance exclusions, and offboarding/export procedure.

**Readiness:** **Requires capability development and a pilot.** The current evidence does not support 24/7 support, guaranteed response times, or an unbounded optimization retainer.

**CTA after a successful build:** “Plan ongoing ownership.” Do not promote this as a launch-page primary offer until the support model is real.

## Services not recommended for launch

- ISO/IEC 42001 implementation or certification readiness.
- Legal/regulatory compliance assessments.
- Security penetration testing or formal red teaming.
- High-stakes HR, credit, healthcare, legal, financial, or safety decision automation.
- Fully autonomous customer commitments, pricing, refunds, collections, or scheduling where context or customer preference requires a person.
- Enterprise-wide “AI transformation.”

Use qualified partners for legal, privacy, cybersecurity, certification, and regulated-industry work. Framework familiarity is not a credential.

### FAQ

**Do you only build with AI?**  
No. We choose between process change, existing software, conventional automation, and AI based on the work. AI is useful where interpretation or flexible language matters; fixed rules are often better for deterministic steps.

**Can you guarantee how many hours we will save?**  
No. We establish a baseline and agree on measurement before making a client-specific estimate. Recovered capacity is not automatically cash savings.

**Will this replace an employee?**  
That is not our default goal or claim. Most useful projects remove repeated administration, improve handoffs, or support decisions while people retain responsibility.

**Can you work with our current software?**  
We assess it first. Available integrations, account permissions, data quality, vendor limits, and cost determine what is feasible. Sometimes the best result is better use of what you already own.

**Do you provide compliance or security certification?**  
No. We use practical risk, privacy, testing, and access-control disciplines. Formal legal, certification, penetration-testing, and regulated-industry work requires an appropriately qualified specialist.

**Who owns the finished workflow?**  
The proposal identifies ownership and licensing. Our default recommendation is client-owned service accounts, editable documentation, and a clear offboarding path. Third-party platforms retain their own terms.

## Page 3 — How We Work

**Purpose:** Turn reliability into a visible method rather than an unsupported adjective.

**Page title:** How We Design Dependable AI Workflows | Wonder & Workflow  
**Meta description:** See how Wonder & Workflow maps, simplifies, builds, tests, and hands over one business workflow with clear human ownership and fallback.

### Hero

**Eyebrow:** The Wonder & Workflow method

**Headline:** The system around the AI matters more than the demo.

**Supporting copy:** A promising output is not an operating process. Reliable work needs defined inputs, ownership, tests, exceptions, monitoring, and a manual path when technology is unavailable.

### Step 1 — Understand the real work

We speak with the person doing the work, follow a recent example, identify the source of truth, and include the awkward cases. We separate touch time from waiting, symptoms from causes, and ambition from present capability.

**You receive:** Current-state map, baseline, problem statement, and open questions.

### Step 2 — Choose the simplest suitable change

We compare a clearer procedure, a native software feature, conventional automation, and AI-assisted patterns. We consider consequence of error, variability, data sensitivity, permissions, operating cost, and who can maintain the result.

**You receive:** Options comparison and documented proceed/no-go decision.

### Step 3 — Define control before building

We name the workflow owner, reviewers, backups, approval points, prohibited actions, escalation criteria, maximum queue, and what happens when nobody is available. Access is limited to what the workflow requires.

**You receive:** Decision register, access map, exception plan, and fallback.

### Step 4 — Build and test the edges

We test ordinary cases, incomplete inputs, duplicates, conflicting information, vendor failures, and cases that must go to a person. Release criteria are agreed before the system is judged.

**You receive:** Test set, acceptance report, defect list, and release decision.

### Step 5 — Hand over and measure

We train the operator, document administration, record changes, and establish how volume, overrides, errors, delays, cost, and business results will be reviewed.

**You receive:** Operating guide, administrator notes, training, change log, and measurement sheet.

### Section — What “human in the loop” means here

It means a named person reviews a defined class of cases at a specific point, using written criteria, with authority to approve, correct, escalate, or stop the workflow. The design also accounts for workload, absence, backlog, and automation bias. A person clicking “approve” without context or time is not a control.

### Section — Proportionate, not bureaucratic

Low-consequence drafting may need a quick operator check and version history. A workflow that can send commitments, expose confidential data, or affect a person’s rights needs stronger controls and may require specialist review. We scale the process to the consequence—not to the novelty of the technology.

# 4. What makes AI operations reliable

## Practical practice matrix

| Practice | Buyer problem solved | Necessary vs excessive | Implementation and client deliverable | How to check it | Classification for Wonder & Workflow |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Workflow discovery and “no AI” decision | Automating the wrong process; adding tools without removing friction | Necessary before meaningful integration; excessive when applied as a company-wide transformation to a tiny low-risk task | Current-state map, baseline, options comparison, proceed/no-go record | Workflow owner validates map; option assumptions tested | **Differentiator if consistently delivered** |
| Data and permission map | Wrong source, poor data, overbroad access, unclear retention | Necessary when systems or sensitive data cross boundaries | Source-of-truth register, field map, least-access plan, prohibited inputs, retention decision | Permission test, sample-data review, access review | **Table stakes** |
| Acceptance evaluations | Impressive demo fails on normal or edge cases | Necessary for AI-assisted outputs and automations; scale test depth to consequence | Representative test set, expected outputs, thresholds, defect record, release decision | Re-run versioned tests; record pass/fail and unresolved defects | **Differentiator in small-business market** |
| Human decision design | Rubber-stamp review, backlog, unavailable reviewer, automation bias | Necessary where judgment or consequence remains; excessive for deterministic low-risk transfers with strong validation | Decision register, review criteria, queue, authority, backup, escalation, manual fallback | Override rate, queue age, error/complaint rate, response time, adjudication outcomes | **Differentiator** |
| Least agency and tool boundaries | Prompt injection or error causes unintended actions/data access | Necessary whenever AI can call tools, send, write, delete, buy, or expose data | Action allowlist, scoped credentials, validation, approval gates, sandbox/test mode | Negative tests, permission denial, tool-call logs | **Table stakes; specialist review for high risk** |
| Monitoring and audit trail | Failures are silent; nobody can explain what happened | Necessary for operationally important flows; excessive telemetry can add cost/noise for trivial drafting | Run log, alert rules, exception queue, cost/volume dashboard, change log | Alert drills, log completeness, exception resolution, cost variance | **Table stakes** |
| Fallback, rollback, continuity | Vendor or integration outage stops work | Necessary when work is time-sensitive or business-critical | Manual procedure, pause control, rollback steps, data reconciliation plan | Tabletop or live continuity test | **Differentiator when visibly tested** |
| Knowledge freshness and retrieval quality | AI answers from outdated, missing, or unauthorized material | Necessary for knowledge assistants and document-grounded workflows | Approved corpus, owner, update cadence, citation/source rule, retrieval tests | Freshness checks, answer-support review, known-answer test set | **Table stakes** |
| Training and ownership | Tool is abandoned or misused; consultant becomes permanent bottleneck | Necessary for every client-facing operation | Operator guide, admin guide, training, ownership matrix, change request path | User demonstration, adoption/use, error patterns, owner sign-off | **Table stakes** |
| Change control | Model, prompt, API, field, or policy change breaks behavior | Necessary for live integrations; process can be light for low-risk work | Version record, test-before-release rule, approver, rollback | Test evidence linked to each material change | **Differentiator if disciplined** |
| Incident response | Harm or data exposure grows while roles are unclear | Necessary for systems with external action or sensitive data; lightweight for low-risk drafts | Severity guide, contacts, containment, notification/escalation, learning review | Scenario drill and post-incident actions | **Table stakes; specialist for security/privacy events** |
| Cost control | Usage and subscriptions erase value | Necessary where tasks, tokens, calls, storage, or premium actions are metered | Cost model, limits, alerts, vendor/subscription register | Actual monthly cost vs baseline/forecast and delivered volume | **Table stakes** |
| Governance program | Many AI systems lack consistent accountability | Necessary as system count, risk, regulation, or procurement demands grow; excessive as enterprise bureaucracy for one low-risk workflow | Inventory, policy, roles, risk tiering, review cadence, exceptions | Audit decisions and control operation | **Supporting discipline now; specialist service later** |

NIST’s AI RMF is voluntary and organized around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. Its playbook explicitly calls for documented human oversight, overrides, complaints, response times, adjudication, policy exceptions, escalations, and go/no-go decisions—supporting an operational rather than ceremonial approach to oversight. [NIST AI RMF Playbook—Measure](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/playbook/measure/) The Generative AI Profile is a voluntary cross-sector companion, published July 26, 2024 and updated on the NIST page April 8, 2026. [NIST AI 600-1](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence)

OWASP’s guidance makes prompt injection and excessive agency concrete concerns when language-model systems receive untrusted content or can use tools. The business implication is not to sell fear; it is to bound permissions, validate outputs, require approval for consequential actions, and test attempts to cross those boundaries. [OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/)

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 is a published requirements standard for an AI management system. Certification is voluntary and performed by independent certification bodies—not by ISO and not by a consultant merely familiar with the standard. Wonder & Workflow should not claim certification, compliance, or audit readiness. [ISO/IEC 42001](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001) [ISO explanation of certification](https://www.iso.org/cms/%20render/live/fr/sites/isoorg/home/insights-news/resources/iso-42001-explained-what-it-is.html)

## Human oversight: operating design

For each AI-assisted decision, document:

1. **Object of review:** output, source evidence, proposed action, or exception.
2. **Timing:** before action, after action within a reversible window, or sampled monitoring.
3. **Criteria:** completeness, factual support, customer intent, policy boundary, price/safety/commitment limit, and escalation trigger.
4. **Authority:** approve, edit, reject, pause, reverse, or escalate.
5. **Service capacity:** expected case volume, maximum queue, review window, primary and backup.
6. **Evidence:** input, output, source/version, reviewer, action, timestamp, and reason for override.
7. **Failure path:** what happens when the reviewer, AI service, source system, or integration is unavailable.

Use pre-action review for irreversible, external, financial, safety-sensitive, privacy-sensitive, or rights-affecting actions. Use post-action sampling only where errors are low-consequence, detectable, and reversible. Fully autonomous operation is appropriate only for bounded actions whose failure modes, permissions, validation, recovery, and monitoring justify it.

## Proportionate small-business implementation

For one low-risk internal workflow, a practical minimum is:

- One-page system/workflow record.
- Named owner and backup.
- Approved purpose and prohibited uses.
- Source, permission, retention, and vendor record.
- Representative acceptance tests.
- Exception and manual fallback.
- Basic logging, alerting, and cost tracking.
- Change log and re-test rule.
- Operator training.

Add formal impact assessment, legal review, security testing, stronger audit trails, segregated access, vendor due diligence, and incident procedures when sensitive data, external actions, regulated services, protected classes, safety, money, employment, credit, healthcare, or significant customer impact is involved.

# 6. Buyer and competitor findings

## Demand interpretation

The safest conclusion is not a single adoption percentage. Current sources measure different populations and definitions:

- Census BTOS reported roughly 17–20% overall U.S. business AI use from December 2025 to May 2026 after broadening its question to any business function. Firms under 20 employees did not show a significant increase in that period. [Census, 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)
- NFIB’s 2025 survey reported 24% of small-business owners using AI and found many nonusers saw no near-term need; process automation was a relatively uncommon named use. [NFIB, 2025](https://www.nfib.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/2025-NFIB-Technology-Survey.pdf)
- A U.S. Chamber/Teneo online survey reported 58% generative-AI use among businesses under 250 employees and 47% in construction, with most users relying on externally developed tools. [U.S. Chamber, 2025](https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/Empowering-Small-Business-Report-2025.pdf)
- A vendor-sponsored trades survey found integration and training among the leading barriers, alongside understanding tools and unclear ROI/use cases. [ServiceTitan/Thrive Analytics, 2026 report](https://get.servicetitan.com/rs/304-EAE-038/images/0102038_ST_AI_Report%20v2-4.pdf?version=0)

The different results reflect question wording, samples, and definitions. Publish no universal “X% of small businesses use AI” claim. The commercial insight is stable despite the disagreement: many businesses are experimenting, but selecting a useful workflow, integrating it, training people, and proving value remain unresolved.

Independent OECD survey evidence across seven countries similarly finds that “not suited to the work” is a leading barrier and that AI increases the importance of analysis and interpretation skills. [OECD, *Generative AI and the SME Workforce*, 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/generative-ai-and-the-sme-workforce_2d08b99d-en/full-report.html) A large field experiment with Microsoft 365 Copilot found changes in individually controllable work such as email, but less change in coordination-heavy work, reinforcing the need for process and role redesign. [NBER Working Paper 33795, 2025](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795)

## Best initial segments

1. **AI-curious firms with no implemented workflow:** They need selection and design, not a tool list.
2. **Software-equipped firms with disconnected operations:** They need native-feature optimization, integration, and ownership.
3. **Firms with fragile or abandoned automations:** They need repair, alerts, documentation, exception handling, and continuity.
4. **Owner/admin bottlenecks in 5–50 person businesses:** Promising hypothesis because pain, repeatability, software use, and budget may align; validate through interviews.

Solo operators remain a possible fit for light configuration, but consulting price competes with SaaS tools costing tens or hundreds of dollars per month.

## Alternatives

- Native platforms such as [Jobber](https://www.getjobber.com/pricing/), [Housecall Pro](https://www.housecallpro.com/pricing/), and [ServiceTitan](https://www.servicetitan.com/get-pricing) already provide scheduling, invoicing, reminders, booking, and AI features. - Conventional process improvement and an internal admin/IT hire are valid alternatives. Do not fabricate salary comparisons.

## Buyer objections

Validated survey themes include unclear suitability, no meaningful improvement, cost, complexity, integration, training, and unclear ROI. ## Differentiation that can be substantiated

- Recommend no AI when a simpler option is better.
- Prove one workflow before expanding.
- Define human review as an operating role, not a slogan.
- Test exceptions, duplicates, unavailable reviewers, and fallback.
- Deliver editable maps, tests, operating guides, and change records.
- Keep client accounts and ownership clear.
- Separate measured capacity from cash savings.

These practices are not unique. The opportunity is to make them consistent, visible, and verifiable in a market where many providers emphasize speed, automation volume, and ROI claims.

# 8. Capability, proof, and conversion-validation plan

## Capability ledger

### Can be used now

- One-workflow assessment and focused consultation.
- Structured workflow discovery, baseline collection, risk/fit reasoning, recommendation, and scoped proposal.
- Honest process/native-feature/automation/AI option comparison.
- Bounded internal workflow implementation patterns with durable capture, human review, duplicate handling, and documentation.
- Proposed website copy that avoids unsupported performance claims.

### Requires validation before publication or sale

- Whether the fit review is no-fee or paid.
- Consistent ability to deliver every diagnostic artifact and clarification boundary.
- Exact implementation stack and the systems the team is prepared to support.
- Stabilization period, coverage hours, response expectations, and backup capacity.
- Pricing derived from actual delivery time, third-party costs, overhead, margin, and scope risk.
- Final assessment, scheduling, notification, privacy, and follow-up flow at the production domain.

### Requires proof first

- Any time-saved, error-reduction, revenue, cost, satisfaction, adoption, or payback claim.
- Case studies, testimonials, logos, before/after metrics, and industry specialization claims.
- Workflow Care as a recurring managed service.
- Claims that Wonder & Workflow can support a named platform beyond tested capabilities.

### Requires a specialist or partner

- Legal/regulatory advice and privacy-law determinations.
- ISO certification or formal audit readiness.
- Cybersecurity assessment, penetration testing, incident forensics, and formal vendor-risk assessment.
- Regulated or high-impact AI decisions.
- Complex ERP, on-premises, data-engineering, or custom-software work beyond demonstrated capability.

## Proof plan

For the first three suitable pilots, capture:

1. **Starting condition:** workflow boundary, volume, touch/wait time, rework, exceptions, cost, and user confidence.
2. **Intervention:** exactly what changed, including process and software.
3. **Acceptance:** test cases, results, defects, and release decision.
4. **Operating period:** enough time to observe representative volume and exceptions.
5. **Outcome:** observed changes in cycle time, touches, backlog, rework, missed follow-up, cost, or capacity.
6. **Attribution limits:** other changes, seasonality, staffing, and measurement gaps.
7. **Permission:** written approval for what may be named, quoted, or anonymized.

An honest case study should state the baseline, implementation, measurement window, observed result, limitations, and maintenance obligations. “The client loved it” is not operational proof.

## Customer validation

Conduct 8–12 interviews across solo, 5–15, and 16–50 employee owner-led service firms. Include owners and office/operations managers. Ask about the last:

- Missed handoff or follow-up.
- Repeated entry or document chase.
- Automation that failed or was abandoned.
- Software feature they pay for but do not use.
- Customer interaction they refuse to automate.
- Evidence they would require before paying for a diagnostic or build.

Test three value propositions without claiming superiority:

1. Recover owner/admin capacity.
2. Make one workflow dependable.
3. Repair the system nobody trusts.

Test willingness to pay separately for a short fit review, a professional diagnostic, and a diagnostic fee credited toward implementation. Do not infer demand from compliments; ask for a next-step commitment.

## Conversion measurement

| Stage | Leading indicator | Business outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Qualified traffic | Visits from intended industries/roles; service-page depth | Intended buyers begin the assessment |
| Assessment | Start/completion rate; field abandonment; stated workflow specificity | Qualified assessment with one real problem and owner |
| Scheduling | Assessment-to-booking rate; no-show/cancel rate | Completed consultation |
| Consultation | Problem confirmed; owner present; baseline obtainable; next decision | Diagnostic proposal requested/accepted |
| Diagnostic | Artifacts accepted; proceed/revise/stop decision | Paid implementation approved where justified |
| Implementation | Test pass rate; defects; training completion; readiness decision | Workflow released with owner and fallback |
| Operation | Run success, exception rate, queue age, overrides, cost, adoption | Measured cycle-time/rework/capacity/customer outcome |

Do not label the copy “high-converting” until it has qualified-visitor and opportunity data. Use event names that reflect real steps, not vanity clicks. Review conversion by traffic source, buyer segment, workflow type, and readiness—not only total volume.

## Highest-value decisions for the founders

1. Confirm the initial buyer: trades/field-service and adjacent service firms, and whether the 5–50 employee band is the primary test segment.
2. Decide the entry economics: complimentary Fit Review, paid Fit Review, or direct paid Diagnostic.
3. Define actual delivery capacity: platforms supported, weekly project load, stabilization window, backup coverage, and when a specialist is required.
4. Choose whether the current assessment remains seven scored questions or becomes a shorter non-scored workflow intake.
5. Approve a public proof policy: no testimonial or metric without source, method, permission, and limitations.

## Research limits and stopping rationale

Research used authoritative standards/regulatory sources, current government and independent business evidence, a justified niche competitor sample, adjacent consultancies, embedded-software alternatives, and anecdotal objection signals. Searches stopped when each consequential recommendation had primary or clearly qualified support, competitor patterns converged, adoption disagreement was explained rather than averaged away, and further broad results repeated generic automation claims. Remaining gaps—customer willingness to pay, founder capacity, live-site state, and client outcomes—require primary business validation rather than more web research.
